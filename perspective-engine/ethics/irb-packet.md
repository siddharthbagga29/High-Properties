## 4. Debrief scripts
Principle: every session ends with a debrief covering strengths, variability between people and accommodations. All text below is a DRAFT for advisors to rewrite, shorten or veto (per V04, no first content review happens before a debrief draft exists; this is it). Strengths statements are authored by advisors, not by us. No empirical claims about strengths are made here. Delivered on screen with an audio and text-only option.

### 4.1 T-arm session debrief (shown immediately after the simulation, before the planning step)
- **D1 Check in.** "That is the end of the simulation. How are you doing? [ ] Fine [ ] A bit unsettled [ ] Upset. Many people feel irritated, uneasy, sad or sorry for others after exercises like this. Those reactions are common and are not what we are aiming for. A Support button is on every screen." If "upset": Support screen with contact (section 5).
- **D2 What this was.** "You used an illustration of four attention and timing patterns that some people with ADHD report or show on average. We set the settings ourselves. They are not measurements, and no simulation can show what ADHD is like. This one does not try to."
- **D3 People differ.** "Group-level patterns do not describe any person. Some people with ADHD notice one of these patterns, some several, some none. How much they matter changes with the task, the setting and the support around. ADHD is not only these patterns, and many people have other conditions too." [Advisors to add their own words.]
- **D4 Strengths.** "[TO FILL: 3-4 short statements written by lived-experience advisors, with their consent, on interests, skills and ways of working. Advisors decide the wording and framing and may veto it; avoid inspiration framing; see 4.5 for how this fits the requirement that every debrief covers strengths.]"
- **D5 Two-way communication.** "When people work differently, both sides can adjust: the manager, the colleague and the setting. The fix does not sit with one person." [Design principle, not a research claim; advisors to check. Do not cite the double empathy literature as established for ADHD: partners.md section 3, group 3 entry (Tizard/Kent "Ask") says extending double empathy to ADHD is an extrapolation, and the group 2 entry (Edinburgh "Limit") says the evidence is autism-specific and ADHD double-empathy evidence is unverified.]
- **D6 Accommodations.** "Many workplaces use practices that suit everyone, such as agendas ahead of meetings or written follow-ups. We are studying whether managers adopt them; we have not shown that each one helps. The best step is to ask people what helps them, without making anyone disclose anything."
- **D7 What not to take away.** "This is not a way to diagnose or guess who on your team has ADHD. It is not proof you now understand it. Pity is not the goal. One short session cannot replace listening to the people you work with."
- **D8 Next and support.** "Next is a short planning step. If you want to talk to someone about how you feel, here is [TO FILL: contact and resources]."

### 4.2 C-arm closing (after the information module and planning step)
Contains D3 (people differ), D4 (strengths), D6 (accommodations) and D7 (what not to take away), so that this arm is built to end with all three required elements once D4 is filled (4.5). D3 and D4 use the same wording as in 4.1, and D4 is subject to 4.5. Why D3 and D4 are included: the information module gives group-level facts, and without D3 it would invite the same stereotype hardening this study monitors; leaving them out of one arm would also make the arms differ in more than the simulation. Effect on the design: both arms now share these caveats, so the pilot estimates what the simulation adds on top of them, not the simulation against nothing. Not repeated here: D1 and D2 (they refer to the simulation); D5 (two-way communication), left out so the arms stay distinct and because it is not one of the three required elements (advisors may add it to both arms). Placement relative to the T1 survey should match the T-arm debrief: open item for Curie (section 9). Then: "This session gave information only. Other participants are doing an interactive session; at the end of the study we will tell you which group you were in and why. If anything here left you uneasy, here is [TO FILL: contact]."

### 4.5 Strengths requirement and the advisor veto (proposal; founder and advisors to confirm)
Two rules meet at D4. Rule A: every session ends with a debrief covering strengths, variability between people and accommodations (Milton's standard). Rule B: paid neurodivergent advisors hold a veto over simulation content, which in this packet includes the debrief. If advisors decide that no strengths statement should be used, B can defeat A. Proposed handling:
1. Authorship. D4 is written only by advisors, with their consent. We do not write a stand-in, and the draft carries no empirical claim about strengths. The same D4 text is used in 4.1, 4.2 and 4.3.
2. No empty slot. A build cannot be frozen with D4 as a [TO FILL]. At freeze, D4 is either (a) advisor-approved text, or (b) a recorded advisor decision not to use strengths framing, with their reasons. Advisors are not yet seated, so today no D4 text exists and the draft debrief does not yet meet Rule A.
3. Veto prevails on wording and framing. If advisors choose (b), the debrief ships without a strengths statement; nobody overrides them and nobody substitutes our own wording. Variability (D3) and accommodations (D6) stay, unless advisors rewrite them too.
4. The gap is made visible, not hidden. A (b) decision is recorded in the hold log, shown to the founder and the IRB, and stated in the write-up as a deviation from the project's debrief standard, together with the advisors' reasons. Advisors may offer their own replacement wording (for example on skills and ways of working) and then Rule A is met in their words.
5. Our role is to ask, explain why the element is there, and warn against inspiration or "superpower" framing; not to press. [FOUNDER TO CONFIRM that the advisor decision outranks the strengths requirement.]

## 7. Risk-benefit statement
**Our assessment (for the IRB to accept or revise):** risk to individual participants is low to moderate, mostly psychological (frustration, unease, pity, helplessness) and not otherwise expected to exceed everyday discomfort; the larger uncertainty is indirect: managers could treat colleagues worse (for example more pity, more stereotype) after a simulation. The literature gives a reason to take this seriously, but only indirectly: Nario-Redmond et al. 2017 ran two experiments with university students (Experiment 1 N=60, Experiment 2 N=50) and report, in both, that disability simulations left participants more confused, embarrassed and helpless; in Experiment 2 only, participants also judged themselves less competent, expressed more pity and more interaction discomfort, and were not more willing to interview disabled students for an accessibility project (see section 0 for how this was checked). They did not measure how anyone treats colleagues, and they were not workplace studies of this product. Likelihood and size for this simulation are unknown; this study exists to find out, and the pause rules respond to it.

| Risk | Who | Mitigation | Monitoring |
|---|---|---|---|
| Distress, frustration, sensory discomfort | Managers, esp. those with ADHD | Content note, intensity ceiling, stop/pause, advisor veto, debrief, support page | Distress item, stop events, section 5 |
| Pity, stereotype hardening, helplessness | Managers, and later their colleagues | Debrief D3-D7; no diagnosis or labelling; advisor review | QPS subscale, AQ-27, self-efficacy; T-arm pause rule |
| Third-party harm | Neurodivergent colleagues who did not consent | Practices apply to everyone; no one is identified; harm outcomes measured | Direct-report concern route; site suspension |
| Retaliation or exposure | Direct reports | Anonymous, 3-respondent rule, no demographics, never shown to manager | Level 3 reporting |
| Privacy and re-identification | All | Key held by university; no company access to coded data; n>=10; redaction; no content-level logs | Section 6 |
| Coercion, undue influence | Managers | Site agreement; no roster; no performance link; separate commercial contacts | Concerns channel |
| Conflict of interest | Study integrity | Independent blinded analyst; frozen script; report nulls | Deviation log |
| Misleading expectations | Managers | No benefit promised; claims check (section 9) | Advisor review |
| Time burden | Managers, direct reports | Short surveys; self-paced | Attrition |

**Benefits.** Direct: none guaranteed. Both arms receive a menu of practices and a planning step; practical value is possible but unproven. Offer of the simulation after the study, if approved [FOUNDER TO CONFIRM]. Indirect: evidence on whether a simulation sold to employers adds anything beyond information, and whether it harms. A pre-registered, active-control, harm-monitored design is itself of public interest because simulations of this kind are used with little testing (unverified how widely).

**Balance.** Uncertainty about benefit and harm justifies comparing against information (genuine uncertainty, not assumption of benefit), provided the content has advisor approval, participants can leave freely, harm is monitored with a real stop rule, and no one is told it will help. If the IRB finds risk higher than we have estimated, we would rather change or drop the T-arm than narrow the monitoring.
