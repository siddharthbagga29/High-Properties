Insurance stopped
covering the machine.

In January 2026 the standard American business liability form gained an off-the-shelf switch that removes cover for anything an AI does. Sentinel measures whether a company's AI behaves — and then insures the ones that can prove it.

Businesses are handing decisions to AI. Their insurance is quietly being rewritten so it no longer pays when that AI gets something wrong. We check the AI, score it, watch it continuously, and sell insurance to the companies whose AI passes.

Every number on this page was checked against a court record, a regulator, a statistical agency or a named company. The ones that failed the check are shown too — struck out, with the reason.

01 — The business in one page

Three sentences, no jargon.

If you read nothing else, read these.

We measure

Paid assessments and continuous telemetry that record what a customer's AI actually did — every output, every refusal, every drift from its tested behaviour — against the ISO/IEC 42001 management standard.

We install the equivalent of a flight recorder on a company's AI.

We certify

That record becomes a defensible artefact: a score a board can sign, a buyer can demand in procurement, and a regulator can inspect.

We turn that recording into a certificate their customers and regulators will accept.

Then we insure

Only monitored customers can buy the cover. The telemetry is the underwriting file, which is the one thing every competitor in this market is missing.

Because we already know how their AI behaves, we can price insurance on it that nobody else can price. That is the whole advantage.

Why this order matters more than anything else on this page

An insurer will not hand a new company the pen without loss data. You cannot get loss data without customers. You cannot get customers for insurance nobody has priced. The measurement business breaks that loop: it is sellable on day one, it needs no regulator and no carrier, and it manufactures exactly the data the insurance leg requires. Our simulation puts the correct sequence at 2.3× the odds of reaching the full stack versus doing insurance first.

Sell the measuring tape before you sell the insurance. You need the measurements to price the insurance at all.

03 — What AI failure actually costs

Five orders of magnitude.

These are real, settled or adjudicated outcomes — not projections. The spread between the smallest and largest is a factor of two million, which is precisely why no insurer can price this line from intuition.

Below are actual amounts real companies actually paid when their AI went wrong. The smallest was $651. The largest was $1.5 billion. That enormous gap is the problem — and the reason measurement is worth paying for.

The underwriter's actual problem

A line of insurance needs a loss distribution. Here the observed losses run from three figures to ten, the frequency is compounding at roughly 55% a year (Stanford HAI recorded 233 AI incidents in 2024 and 362 in 2025), and there is no credible history. Faced with that, a rational carrier either excludes the risk — which is what ISO's January 2026 forms let them do — or demands evidence per risk. Sentinel sells the evidence.

Insurers price things by looking at what happened before. For AI, there is no "before". So they are switching the cover off instead. Someone has to build the "before" — that is the business.

04 — Corrections

What the research killed.

An earlier version of this thesis rested on four figures and one assumption that did not survive verification. They are listed here rather than quietly removed, because a strategy is only as good as the things it refuses to believe.

We had a story. We checked it. Parts of it were wrong, so we changed the plan. Here is exactly what changed and why.

Claims that failed verification and what replaced them
We had believedWhat verification foundConsequence
AI liability insurance is a $6.8bn market in 2025, reaching $34.2bn by 2034. Traceable only to syndicated report vendors with no methodology. Munich Re measures all of global cyber insurance — a 25-year-old line — at $15.3bn in 2024. A standalone AI line at $6.8bn would already be 44% of that, while the category's biggest writers offer per-risk limits of $9.25m–$50m. Discarded. Pool re-anchored bottom-up at $60m–$420m of global standalone GWP in 2026.
74% of small businesses use AI. Real, but it is HSB's own marketing survey of 1,000 firms, published to launch a product. The US Census Bureau's probability sample (BTOS) puts actual AI use at 19.8% as of May 2026 — under 20% for firms with four or fewer employees. Buyer base cut by roughly a factor of four. Weakens every volume-led plan.
Regulation will force purchase on a known date. The opposite happened, twice in twelve months. The EU's Digital Omnibus deal (7 May 2026) moved Annex III high-risk obligations from 2 Aug 2026 to 2 Dec 2027. Colorado's AI Act slipped from Feb to June 2026, was then blocked by a federal magistrate on 27 Apr 2026, and its replacement targets Jan 2027. Regulatory timing is now modelled as a 45%-per-year slip, not a tailwind. Any plan whose revenue needs a deadline is fragile.
The small-business wedge is open. Munich Re's HSB launched AI Liability Insurance for SMBs on 18 March 2026, distributed white-label inside partner carriers' existing policies. An SME-embedded MGA cannot out-distribute Munich Re inside incumbent paper. That strategy is dead.
Only five AI liability products exist worldwide. Sourced to a blog post. Our own enumeration from trade press found at least eight identifiable writers plus the Google Cloud endorsement route. "First mover" is not available. Replaced with a specific, defensible wedge.

Short version: the market is smaller than the internet claims, far fewer businesses use AI than surveys suggest, the laws that were meant to force people to buy have been delayed, and the biggest reinsurer in the world already took the easy customers. So we picked a different, harder, more defensible plan.

05 — Simulation

Sixty thousand futures.

Three strategies, five years, 60,000 Monte Carlo trials each, re-anchored on the verified inputs above. Success is defined strictly: the business still holds carrier capacity, clears $10m of year-five revenue, and is cumulatively cash-positive.

We ran the business plan sixty thousand times with different luck each time, then counted how often it worked. Three versions of the plan were tested. One of them wins by a distance.

The uncomfortable finding we are not hiding

In the winning model, insurance is only about 10% of year-five revenue — roughly $5m of $71m at the median. The software carries the business. Insurance is not the revenue engine; it is the moat, the reason the software is defensible, and the reason a customer cannot churn to a cheaper monitoring tool. Anyone pitching this as an insurance company first is describing the 2.4% outcome.

Most of the money comes from the software, not the insurance. The insurance is what stops competitors copying the software.

      So what is genuinely unoccupied?

      Not the insurance. Every player above is selling a policy against an underwriting judgement made largely at inception. None of them holds continuous behavioural telemetry on the insured system — a live record of what the model did every day of the policy period.       Everyone is selling the policy. Nobody is watching the AI after the policy is signed. That gap is where we go.

      08 — Elements required for market success

      What has to be true.

      Derived from the tornado, not from ambition. These are ordered by how much they actually move the probability of success.

      The five things that decide whether this works — in order of how much each one matters.

      1 · Loss selection discipline 15.4 pts

      The single largest swing. Attach cover only to monitored customers, and decline the ones whose telemetry looks wrong even when they want to pay. A bad loss year does not cost you margin — it costs you the pen, and 11.9% of simulated runs end exactly that way.

      Refuse to insure the risky customers, even when they wave money. One bad year and the insurer behind us walks away.

      2 · Cost of acquisition 10.2 pts

      At ~$14k blended, the model works. At $22k it stops working. That forces a channel answer rather than a sales-headcount answer: auditors, cloud marketplaces and broker panels who already own the relationship.

      Winning customers has to be cheap. That means partners doing the selling, not an expensive sales team.

      3 · Contract value 7.4 pts

      Land at assessment prices, expand to continuous monitoring, then attach cover. The path from a $25k audit to a six-figure monitored relationship is the whole compounding story.

      Start with a small paid check-up, grow it into an ongoing subscription, then add insurance on top.

      4 · Capital efficiency 5.2 pts

      Median capital to break even is $6.1m, against $32m for the SME MGA route and $81m for the enterprise MGA route. Staying software-shaped is what keeps the number small.

      This plan needs about $6m. The insurance-first plans need $32m to $81m for worse odds.

      5 · Independence from the calendar

      Regulatory slippage swings the outcome by 0.5 points. That is the design goal, not a happy accident: the product must be bought for operational reasons — incident reduction, procurement pass-through, board assurance — and any regulatory deadline that survives is upside rather than the thesis. Both major deadlines slipped this year; a plan that needed them would already be broken.

      Never build a business that only works if a law arrives on time. Two of them were late this year alone.
